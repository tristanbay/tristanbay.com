---
title: "A nonlinear measure of speed"
date: 2026-09-27
---

A while ago, I had this idea for a measure of speed that was nonlinear (and non-exponential and non-logarithmic). The idea is that it would be more sensitive at both ends of the range of possible speeds (stationary and the speed of light in a vacuum, *c*). This way, it would be useful in situations where you have both fast and slow things It's quite possible that someone else has already publicly shared this idea and/or given the same or similar measure of speed, but I'm posting about this since I independently thought of them.  
  
The equation is:  
k = log~10~(*s*) - log~10~(1 - *s*)  
...where k is the nonlinear speed measure, and where *s* is the speed in terms of *c*. *s* can also be expressed as km/h divided by 1079252848, or m/s divided by 299792458.  
  
Here are some real-life speeds converted to this nonlinear speed measure:  
  
- -8.6 is walking speed
- -7.5 is about the speed of a car on a highway
- -4.4 is approximately Earth's escape velocity
- 0 is exactly half of *c*
- 1.0 is about the speed of a 1 amp electrical signal (according to a quick web search)
- 8.0 is about the speed at which particles have been known to travel around the Large Hadron Collider (also according to some web searching)
  
At the very extreme ends is where things break down a bit, though. As an object loses speed, its value in this scale tends toward negative infinity, and there is no defined real number value for standstill (no speed). One option is to use the symbol "∅" to mean "stationary" for this scale. Likewise, *c* is also undefined as a real number in this scale, so we could just call it "*c*".  
  
Not sure how useful this would be scientifically or otherwise, but I thought it was interesting enough to share here.
